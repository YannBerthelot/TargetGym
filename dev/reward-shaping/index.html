    Reward shaping¶

    TargetGym asks one question: can a learned policy hold a setpoint better than a PID or an MPC? That question lives in the reward, so the reward is the measuring instrument, and this page says how it is built.

    Every environment scores through the version-2 reward described here. Twenty normalise the tracking error by an achievable floor; four (reactor, battery, wind turbine, building) put tracking and consumption in the owner's currency, where the floor enters as the reference cost rho_floor instead. Version 1 is described briefly at the end.

    The reward, in one line¶

    reward = -( tracking_cost + running_cost + failure_cost )

    Three non-negative costs, added rather than multiplied, each in a unit the plant's owner could defend. target_gym.reward holds the arithmetic; every environment's compute_reward_terms returns the three terms and compute_reward is minus their sum.

    The best achievable per-step reward is about -1 per tracked output on the dimensionless plants (tracking at the floor, nothing avoidable consumed), and minus the unavoidable bill on the priced ones. The reward is not capped, and its per-step scale differs by orders of magnitude between plants. To compare across plants, use the normalised expert advantage and the two costs reported separately (target_gym.eval), not the raw return.

    Tracking: the error in units of what is achievable¶

    tracking_cost = ( max(|e| - e_tol, 0) / e_floor ) ** p
    • e_floor is the achievable floor: the smallest long-run mean |error| any controller can hold on this plant under its shipped reference and disturbances, but never finer than the instrument the plant cites can resolve (the simulator has no measurement noise, so it can hold finer than a real transmitter reads). An error at the floor costs 1 per step, so a tracking cost reads directly as "how many floor-widths off".
    • e_tol is a specification tolerance where the plant has one (a comfort band, a purity spec, a permit limit). The cost is zero inside it, so inside the band only consumption is optimised. A band must be small against the errors controllers actually make, or it hides them; the aircraft's ±30 m band was dropped for that reason.
    • p is 1 where the owner's cost is linear in the error (an energy imbalance settled per MWh) and 2 otherwise. Multi-output plants sum one term per output, each with its own floor.

    The cost is convex in |e| by construction. Version 1's log-scaled tracking was concave, and a concave cost prefers rare large excursions to frequent small ones of the same mean: the wrong preference for a hold. The Target-MDP note (Proposition 8) gives an example where that preference costs an arbitrary amount.

    Running cost: only what could have been avoided¶

    Consumption (fuel, energy, boilup, reagent, actuator travel) is charged above c_hold, what the best shipped controller consumes per step while holding. That is the avoidable part. Dimensionless form:

    running_cost = w * max(c - c_hold, 0) / c_hold

    w reads as "one floor-width of tracking error is worth w times the hold-phase consumption", default 1, swept over {0.5, 1, 2} where the number matters. Where the owner's tariff is known the term is priced instead (price * quantity), and tracking is in the same currency, so there is no weight to choose: the reactor's imbalance at three times spot, the building's gas per kWh, the battery's imbalance per MWh and fade per kWh of lost capacity. Wear and fatigue have no defensible tariff, so they are charged per unit of avoidable activity times a documented weight.

    Failure¶

    Leaving the operating envelope trips the plant. A trip does not end the episode: the step that leaves the envelope is charged the trip cost, restart_steps * failure_cost, with that step's tracking and running cost zeroed, and the plant restarts at once as reset_env would (base.failure_kernel, reward.trip_cost). terminated stays false, so the restart is an ordinary transition with a cost, as continuing and average-reward methods want it, and every window is the same length.

    • restart_steps is the time a real restart takes, a provisional number a plant engineer would supply (a SCRAM's xenon-limited restart, a furnace heat-up). Each plant's PHYSICS.md gives its value and source.
    • failure_cost is twice the largest tracking cost the plant can reach (its target range against its trip bounds), per step.
    • A plant that does not restart cold (the building, whose thermal mass persists through a lockout) has restart_in_place = True: it keeps its state, and every step outside the envelope is charged until the controller brings it back.

    The long-run cost then decomposes as rho_hold + p * B + lambda * (restart_steps * failure_cost + B_restart), with lambda the trip rate, which the protocol reports separately. The trip is reported as info["tripped"] and the charge as info["trip_cost"]. Two plants never trip: the pH loop and the CSTR cannot reach their limits, which are documentation only.

    Episodic use (optional). With continuing = False a trip ends the episode instead: terminated is raised on that step, and the reward and info are the same. The trip cost then also stops a discounted learner from crashing on purpose (a terminal state worth zero would otherwise be the cheapest state), as long as restart_steps >= 1 / (2 (1 - gamma)): 50 steps at gamma = 0.99, which every plant clears, and 500 at gamma = 0.999, which first_order, cstr and distillation do not.

    Learning with trips¶

    The trip cost prices real downtime, so it is large: from 8 to over 80,000 steps of the worst tracking cost, depending on the plant. On the step it lands it can be orders of magnitude above every other reward, and a learner that does not normalise its targets sees little else. Remedies that leave the objective unchanged:

    • Treat the trip cost as its own channel. info["trip_cost"] is the lump the step charged (0 on every other step). Subtract it from the reward to learn the tracking value, learn or normalise the trip part separately, and add the two back.
    • Use scale-invariant value targets. PopArt (van Hasselt et al., 2016) and similar adaptive normalisation keep the value targets well scaled without changing what is optimal.

    Do not clip the reward. Clipping caps the trip at the size of an ordinary step, so a policy that trips often can outscore a safe one.

    Why the floor: loop performance assessment, in cost units¶

    The floor-normalised cost is the reinforcement-learning form of control-loop performance assessment. Harris (1989) showed that a loop's minimum-variance bound can be estimated from routine closed-loop data, and that the ratio of the actual variance to that bound, the Harris index, tells an engineer how much of the variance the controller is responsible for. Desborough and Harris (1992) turned it into the measures industrial loop auditing uses, Huang and Shah (1999) extended it to multivariable and LQG benchmarks, and Jelali (2006) surveys its industrial uptake.

    e_floor is that bound computed on the simulator rather than estimated from plant data. The benchmark's score is a Harris-type index too, in decades of cost rather than cost: how far a policy has moved from the expert toward tracking within the instrument's resolution (RL protocol, section 4). rho_floor is the lowest per-seed hold cost the oracle demonstrated, in the reward's units; the score does not use it. The two-cost report, tracking against consumption, is Huang and Shah's LQG performance curve: the trade-off frontier a controller is judged against.

    • T. J. Harris (1989). Assessment of control loop performance. The Canadian Journal of Chemical Engineering 67(5), 856-861.
    • L. Desborough and T. Harris (1992). Performance assessment measures for univariate feedback control. The Canadian Journal of Chemical Engineering 70(6), 1186-1197.
    • B. Huang and S. L. Shah (1999). Performance Assessment of Control Loops: Theory and Applications. Springer, Advances in Industrial Control.
    • M. Jelali (2006). An overview of control performance assessment technology and industrial applications. Control Engineering Practice 14(5), 441-466.

    How each floor was obtained¶

    scripts/measure_hold.py runs the shipped PID and MPC on every plant through a burn-in of three cost-bearing time constants and then a hold window, under the shipped reference and disturbances. It records the long-run mean |error| per output, the consumption per step and the settling time after a target change (src/target_gym/data/hold_measurements/). Three kinds of floor come out of it:

    • Certified or closed-form, where the hold problem has a computable optimum: the reactor (scripts/floor_reactor_hold.py, a dynamic programme on the error against the demand's random walk) and the battery (white dispatch noise drawn after the action, so E|error| >= sd * sqrt(2/pi)). The shipped MPC holds at these floors within sampling error.
    • Upper bounds, where no reduced-model optimum exists yet: the best shipped controller's own long-run hold, labelled as such in the plant's PHYSICS.md. A learner that beats it scores a tracking cost below 1, which is allowed and informative.
    • Documented minima on the plants with no disturbance at all (the CSTR, first-order plant and four-tank), where the achievable error is zero and a floor of zero would make the cost unbounded. The instrument resolution is kept as the scale there.

    A test (tests/test_reward_contract.py) checks every measured floor: the shipped MPC's long-run tracking cost must sit at or above the floor's cost, within sampling error. A floor the MPC beats by more is wrong. Each plant's PHYSICS.md gives its floor's derivation.

    Version 1¶

    Version 1, the reward of the 0.6 release, was a log-scaled tracking term capped to [0, 1] and multiplied by one minus a running cost. It is still constructible with reward_version=1 on any params, and its baselines are kept in src/target_gym/data/baseline_returns_v1.json. Version 2 replaced it for three reasons: its concave tracking preferred rare large excursions, its product charged nothing for consumption exactly where tracking was worst, and a trip ended the episode, so its price depended on when it happened. The CHANGELOG records the change.
